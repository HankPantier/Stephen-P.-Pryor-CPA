A contractor in Port Chester pulls into a dealership lot on Boston Post Road looking at a new F-250, and the salesperson leads with the monthly payment. Lease it for $650 a month, or buy it for $850. That's the wrong question. The real question is what the truck does for your business, how many miles it racks up, and what happens to your tax bill and cash flow over the next five years. Get that framework right and the decision usually becomes obvious.

We work with a lot of clients who buy vehicles for the business, from general contractors running crews between job sites to real estate agents showing properties across Westchester and Fairfield counties to restaurant owners who need a reliable van for deliveries and supply runs. The lease-versus-buy call looks different for each of them, and it should. Here's the framework we actually use.

## Start with how the vehicle gets used, not the payment

Before any tax talk, answer three questions honestly. How many miles will this vehicle rack up in a year? Will the business use stay above 50 percent, which the IRS requires for most of the good depreciation breaks? And do you tend to keep vehicles for three years or ten?

A contractor putting 25,000 miles a year on a pickup, hauling tools and materials between job sites, is a different case than a real estate agent who drives a sedan mostly for client meetings and open houses. High mileage and heavy wear favor buying, because leases penalize you hard for exceeding 10,000 to 15,000 miles a year and for anything beyond normal wear. If you're the type of owner who keeps a work truck for eight years and drives it into the ground, leasing is the expensive way to do that.

## The tax math behind Section 179 versus lease deductions

This is where the dealership pitch usually stops short, and where it actually gets interesting for a business owner.

If you buy a vehicle and use it more than 50 percent for business, you may be able to deduct a large portion of the purchase price in the year you put it in service, under [Section 179 of the tax code](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p946). Heavy pickups and SUVs over 6,000 pounds gross vehicle weight get a more generous cap than passenger cars, which makes this especially relevant for contractors buying work trucks. The exact dollar limits change from year to year, so we run the current numbers against your income and equipment purchases before you sign anything, not after.

Leasing doesn't give you that upfront deduction, but it isn't a tax dead end either. Lease payments on a vehicle used for business are generally deductible as an ordinary business expense, prorated for business use. There's no big first-year write-off, but the deduction is steady and predictable every single year of the lease. For a business with tight margins and a strong preference for predictable expenses, like a bar or restaurant already managing thin margins on food and labor cost, that steadiness has real value.

The mistake we see most often is a business owner who buys a vehicle, takes the full Section 179 deduction in year one, and then has no deduction left for years two through five, even though the truck is still running up mileage and costing money. Buying can front-load your tax benefit. Leasing spreads it out. Neither is automatically better. It depends on what your income looks like this year versus next year, which is exactly the kind of projection we build during [business tax planning](/services/outsourced-accounting/business-tax).

## Cash flow decides more of this than tax law does

Once the tax picture is on the table, cash flow usually breaks the tie. Buying requires a down payment, or it locks up borrowing capacity if you finance the full amount. Auto loan rates move with the broader credit market, and the [Federal Reserve's Consumer Credit report](https://www.federalreserve.gov/releases/g19/current/) tracks those trends if you want to see where financing costs are headed before you commit to a loan term.

Leasing typically requires less cash upfront and keeps monthly payments lower, which matters if you're a contractor whose cash flow moves in waves between project draws, or a new restaurant owner still building a cushion in the first two years. But lower monthly payments aren't free. You're paying for flexibility and predictability, and at the end of the lease you own nothing. If your business plan calls for reinvesting every spare dollar into growth right now, leasing frees up capital. If you're sitting on a healthy cash position and want an asset on the books, buying builds equity you can eventually sell or trade in.

This is the same conversation we have with clients weighing rental property purchases or equipment financing. The right answer isn't about which option looks cheaper on a single line item. It's about what your full cash position looks like over the next 24 to 36 months, which is the kind of forecasting we do inside [cash management advisory](/services/advisory/cash-management).

## Business use percentage changes everything

A vehicle used 100 percent for business gets treated very differently than one used 60 percent for business and 40 percent for personal errands. The IRS requires a mileage log or comparable record to substantiate the business percentage, and that percentage directly scales both the Section 179 deduction and the deductible portion of lease payments. Skip the log and you're guessing, and guessing on a vehicle deduction is exactly the kind of thing that draws a second look from a tax authority.

Real estate agents and property managers tend to have blended use, since the same car handles showings, personal errands, and the occasional trip to the office. Contractors running a dedicated work truck usually have a cleaner, higher business-use percentage, which makes the Section 179 math more favorable. If you're not sure which bucket you fall into, that's worth a conversation before the purchase decision, not after the first tax return.

## Lease vs. buy at a glance

| Factor | Leasing | Buying |
|---|---|---|
| Upfront cash needed | Lower | Higher, unless fully financed |
| Annual mileage fit | Best under 12,000-15,000 miles/year | Better for high-mileage, heavy-use vehicles |
| Tax treatment | Steady deduction each year, prorated for business use | Potential large first-year deduction under Section 179, subject to annual limits |
| Long-term ownership | None, unless you buy out the lease | Builds equity in the vehicle |
| Best fit | Agents and service businesses with lower mileage, steady cash flow needs | Contractors and businesses with heavy, high-mileage vehicle use |

That table is a starting point, not a verdict. We've seen contractors for whom leasing made more sense because they upgrade trucks every two years and want warranty coverage the whole time. We've seen real estate investors for whom buying made more sense because they wanted the write-off against a high-income year. The framework holds, but the answer is specific to your numbers.

## Common Questions

**Can I deduct 100 percent of my car payment if I use it for business?**
Only if the vehicle is used 100 percent for business, which is rare outside of a dedicated work truck or delivery van. Most owners deduct a percentage based on business mileage versus total mileage, tracked with a mileage log, and that percentage applies to both loan interest or lease payments and to depreciation.

**Does Section 179 apply to a leased vehicle?**
No. Section 179 requires ownership, so it applies to purchased vehicles, not leased ones. Leased vehicles get their business-use portion deducted as a regular operating expense each year instead, spread across the term of the lease rather than concentrated upfront.

**What happens to my deduction if my business use drops below 50 percent later?**
If business use falls below 50 percent in a later year after taking Section 179, you may have to recapture part of the deduction as income. This is one of the more common surprises we help clients avoid by projecting business use trends before the purchase, not after.

**Is a heavy pickup truck treated differently than a passenger car for tax purposes?**
Yes. Vehicles over 6,000 pounds gross vehicle weight, common among work trucks used in construction, avoid the lower luxury-auto depreciation caps that apply to most passenger cars, which is why so many contractors lean toward buying rather than leasing their primary work vehicle.
