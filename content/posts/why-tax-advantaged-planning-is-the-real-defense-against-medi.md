A medical crisis rarely announces itself. One week you're fine, the next you're staring at a $14,000 hospital bill or a diagnosis that means months of treatment. According to the Federal Reserve's [Report on the Economic Well-Being of U.S. Households](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/report-economic-well-being-us-households.htm), a large share of adults would struggle to cover a $400 emergency expense without borrowing or selling something. Medical bills are a common trigger for exactly that kind of gap, and an emergency fund alone doesn't close it fast enough.

The advice you've heard a hundred times, build a three-to-six-month cushion, isn't wrong. It's just incomplete. A savings account earns nothing in tax benefit and does nothing to reduce what you owe the IRS. The clients who come through a medical crisis in the best financial shape are the ones who [set up tax-advantaged tools](/resources/why-tax-advantaged-planning-is-the-real-defense-against-medi-2) before the crisis hits, not after. That's the real difference between having cash on hand and having a strategy.

## Why an emergency fund isn't enough on its own

Cash reserves matter, but they're a blunt instrument. If you're pulling $10,000 from a regular savings account to cover surgery, that money was earned, taxed, and saved with no offset. Compare that to money you never paid tax on in the first place, sitting in a Health Savings Account, ready to cover the same bill. Same expense, very different tax outcome.
There's also a timing problem. Emergency funds get built slowly, often over years, while medical bills show up on their own schedule. Tax-advantaged accounts let you front-load some of that protection immediately, through payroll contributions or catch-up funding before year-end, in a way a savings account can't match.

## HSA tax benefits are the strongest tool most people underuse

If you're on a high-deductible health plan, a Health Savings Account is close to the best deal in the tax code. Contributions are deductible (or pre-tax through payroll), the account grows tax-free, and withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are never taxed. That's a triple benefit no other account offers, not a 401(k), not a Roth IRA.
For 2024, individuals can contribute up to $4,150 and families up to $8,300, with an extra $1,000 catch-up allowed at 55 and older. Unlike an FSA, HSA funds roll over indefinitely and stay with you if you change jobs. Left untouched, an HSA can function as a second retirement account, since after age 65 you can withdraw funds for any purpose without penalty (though non-medical withdrawals are taxed as income).
The mistake we see most often: people treat the HSA like a checking account, spending contributions as fast as they go in. If your cash flow allows it, paying smaller medical bills out of pocket and letting the HSA balance grow gives you a larger tax-free cushion for the expense that actually knocks you off balance.

## FSAs work differently, and the deadline pressure is real

A Flexible Spending Account also lets you set aside pre-tax dollars for medical costs, but it comes with a use-it-or-soon-lose-it structure. Most plans allow either a small rollover (capped by the IRS) or a short grace period, and anything beyond that reverts to your employer. That makes an FSA a good match for predictable annual costs, orthodontia, contacts, a known procedure, but a poor substitute for open-ended emergency protection.
If your employer offers both an FSA and HSA-eligible plan, run the math before enrollment season instead of defaulting to whatever you picked last year. The right choice depends on your deductible, your predicted medical spending, and whether you want the account to double as a savings vehicle.

## The medical expense deduction most people miss

Even without an HSA or FSA, the tax code offers relief after the fact. Under [IRS Publication 502](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p502), you can deduct unreimbursed medical and dental expenses that exceed 7.5% of your adjusted gross income, if you itemize. That threshold sounds high, but a serious medical event, surgery, a hospital stay, extended physical therapy, can clear it fast.
The expenses that qualify go well beyond doctor visits: mileage to appointments, certain home modifications for a medical condition, long-term care premiums, and prescription costs all count. Most people never track these carefully enough to claim them, because nobody expects to itemize until the bills start piling up. Keeping a running log during a health crisis, rather than reconstructing it at tax time, is often the difference between a real deduction and a missed one.
If a health scare intersects with other life decisions, weighing whether to draw down a long-term care policy, for instance, it helps to look at the full financial picture rather than the tax return in isolation. ## Building the plan before the diagnosis, not after

Here's what proactive medical expense planning actually looks like, in order:

| Step | Action | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Confirm HSA eligibility and max out contributions if your plan qualifies | Triple tax benefit, no expiration |
| 2 | Estimate predictable annual costs and fund an FSA only to that amount | Avoids forfeiting unused balances |
| 3 | Track all medical spending, including mileage and premiums | Builds toward the 7.5% AGI deduction threshold |
| 4 | Keep a separate cash reserve for costs neither account covers | Copays, out-of-network care, non-qualified expenses |
| 5 | Revisit entity and payroll setup if you're self-employed | Self-employed health insurance deduction may apply separately |

Business owners in construction and hospitality face a version of this problem that's easy to overlook: inconsistent income makes it harder to predict how much to set aside, and payroll structures affect what's even eligible for pre-tax treatment. If you're running payroll for a crew or a restaurant staff, the plan design decisions you make for your team can affect your own options too. ## Common Questions

**How much should I keep in cash versus an HSA for medical emergencies?**
A reasonable split is a smaller cash reserve, roughly one month of essential expenses, for immediate copays and non-qualified costs, with the rest of your medical safety net built through HSA contributions if you're eligible. The HSA dollars stretch further because they're never taxed going in or coming out for qualified expenses.

**Can I deduct medical expenses if I don't itemize?**
No. The medical expense deduction under [IRS Publication 502](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p502) only applies if your itemized deductions, including medical costs above 7.5% of your adjusted gross income, exceed the standard deduction. Track expenses anyway; a major medical year can be the one year itemizing makes sense.

**What happens to unused HSA funds at the end of the year?**
Nothing. Unlike an FSA, HSA balances roll over every year with no expiration and no cap on how much can accumulate, which is why funding it consistently, even in years with no major medical costs, pays off later.

**Is COBRA or a marketplace premium a deductible medical expense?**
Health insurance premiums generally qualify as deductible medical expenses under IRS rules once you clear the 7.5% AGI threshold, including COBRA premiums in many cases. Self-employed individuals may have a separate, often more favorable, deduction available for health insurance premiums.
