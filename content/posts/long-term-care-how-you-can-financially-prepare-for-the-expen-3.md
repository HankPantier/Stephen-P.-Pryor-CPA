A genuine conversation about long-term care usually starts too late. By the time a parent has had a fall or a diagnosis, the family is choosing between options under pressure, often paying retail prices for care that could have been funded years earlier at a fraction of the cost. Long-term care is not a medical emergency you insure against after the fact. It is a financial exposure you plan for the way you plan for retirement or a mortgage, with time as your biggest advantage.

The numbers make the case on their own. A private room in a nursing home in Westchester County commonly runs well past $14,000 a month, and a home health aide working 40 hours a week can still add up to $60,000 or more a year. Medicare does not cover custodial care, the day-to-day help with bathing, dressing, and eating that most long-term care actually involves. That gap between what Medicare pays and what care costs is where families get caught off guard, and it's exactly the gap this kind of planning is meant to close.

## Why waiting costs more than the premium

Long-term care insurance is medically underwritten, which means your health at the time of application determines both your eligibility and your rate. Someone who applies at 55 with clean bloodwork and no chronic conditions will pay less and have more carriers willing to write the policy than someone who waits until 68 after a cardiac event. Premiums also climb with age at issue, sometimes by 50 percent or more for every five years you wait. The math rewards people who treat this as a decision to make in their late 50s or early 60s, not a to-do item for when a health scare forces the issue.

That said, insurance is only one leg of the stool. Most people we work with end up blending three sources: a long-term care or hybrid life insurance policy, personal savings earmarked specifically for care, and, for veterans and their spouses, a benefit that is frequently overlooked entirely.

## Comparing the three ways to pay

| Funding source | Best fit | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Long-term care insurance | Healthy applicants in their 50s to mid-60s who want costs capped | Premiums can rise over time; underwriting can decline pre-existing conditions |
| Self-funding through savings and investments | High earners with substantial after-tax savings or those who can't get coverage | Requires setting aside a real, dedicated sum, not just "hoping the portfolio holds up" |
| VA Aid and Attendance benefit | Wartime veterans and surviving spouses meeting income and net worth limits | Application and approval can take months; net worth limits are strict |

Self-funding sounds simple, and for some households it's the right call, particularly if a long-term care policy isn't available at a reasonable rate. But self-funding only works if the money is actually earmarked. We've seen retirement accounts that looked comfortable on paper get depleted in three years once a couple needed two different levels of care at the same time. If you're going this route, treat the long-term care reserve as its own bucket, not part of your general retirement number, and revisit it annually the same way you'd review a balanced investment portfolio.

The VA Aid and Attendance benefit is the piece families overlook most often. It's a monthly, tax-free benefit paid on top of a veteran's regular pension to those who need help with daily activities or who are housebound, and it can also extend to a surviving spouse. The eligibility rules look at net worth and unreimbursed medical expenses, so the calculation is specific to your situation, but for a veteran or widow already paying for home health aide services, it can offset a meaningful chunk of the monthly bill. Details on eligibility and how to apply are on the [VA's Aid and Attendance and Housebound benefits page](https://www.va.gov/pension/aid-attendance-housebound/).

## Where the tax code actually helps

A piece that surprises a lot of clients: certain long-term care expenses and premiums are deductible as medical expenses once they exceed 7.5 percent of adjusted gross income. Premiums on a qualified long-term care insurance policy count toward that deduction up to age-based limits set by the IRS each year, and unreimbursed costs for qualified long-term care services can count too. [IRS Publication 502](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p502) walks through exactly what qualifies, including the distinction between medical care and personal or custodial expenses that don't count. This is also where planning intersects with the broader question of how to plan for unexpected medical expenses using HSAs and other tax-advantaged tools, since the same deduction threshold applies across categories.

If you're self-employed or run a business, there's an added layer worth a real conversation rather than a guess: long-term care premiums paid through certain business structures can be deducible to the business, subject to the same age-based caps. That's a detail worth reviewing alongside your entity structure, not something to assume applies without checking.

## What a realistic plan looks like at different ages

At 50 to 55, the priority is usually getting underwritten while you're healthy, even if you don't buy a policy that year. Getting a quote on record and understanding your rate class gives you real numbers to plan around instead of guesses. At 60 to 65, this is often when people finalize the mix: a policy sized to cover a meaningful share of projected costs, savings earmarked for the rest, and a look at whether a spouse's veteran status opens up Aid and Attendance eligibility down the road. Past 65, if insurance is off the table due to health, the conversation shifts to structuring savings, understanding VA eligibility now rather than later, and making sure adult children know where the paperwork lives.

None of this happens in isolation from the rest of your finances. How you fund long-term care affects your estate plan, your required minimum distributions, and how much you can comfortably draw down each year without running short. It's the kind of decision that benefits from sitting down with someone who can see your full tax picture, not just the insurance quote.

## Common Questions

**Is long-term care insurance worth it if I'm already 65?**
It depends heavily on your health, but premiums at 65 are commonly two to three times what they'd be at 55. If you're in good health, it can still make sense, especially paired with a smaller benefit amount to keep premiums manageable. If health issues have already surfaced, self-funding or exploring hybrid life insurance policies with a long-term care rider is often more realistic.

**Can I deduct long-term care insurance premiums on my taxes?**
Yes, within IRS age-based limits, and only to the extent your total medical expenses exceed 7.5 percent of your adjusted gross income. [IRS Publication 502](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p502) lists the current per-age caps and what qualifies as a deductible long-term care service.

**Does the VA Aid and Attendance benefit cover assisted living or in-home care?**
It can cover both, along with nursing home care, as long as the veteran or surviving spouse meets the medical need and net worth requirements. The benefit is paid directly to the recipient rather than to a facility, so it can be applied toward whichever type of care is being used. Details are on the [VA's benefits page](https://www.va.gov/pension/aid-attendance-housebound/).

**How much should I be saving if I decide to self-fund?**
A common planning benchmark is three to five years of full-time care costs, which in Westchester County can mean $250,000 to $500,000 depending on the level of care needed. That number should be reviewed every year or two as costs and your health picture change, not set once and forgotten.
