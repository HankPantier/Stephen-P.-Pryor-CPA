A slow cooker rewards you for walking away. A stock portfolio doesn't. Leave a 401(k) or brokerage account untouched for five years and the mix of stocks, bonds, and cash you started with stops being the mix you actually have. One sector runs hot, another stalls, and by the time you glance at a statement, a portfolio built for moderate risk has quietly turned aggressive. That drift is the real cost of set-it-and-forget-it investing. It doesn't show up as a bill you can see. It shows up as risk you never agreed to.

For business owners in Port Chester and across Westchester County, this matters more than it might seem. Most people in their 40s and 50s aren't checking their asset allocation strategy every quarter. They're running a restaurant, managing a construction crew, or closing on a rental property. Investments sit in the background until a life event, a market swing, or a tax return forces a second look. By then, the portfolio has usually drifted further than anyone realized.

## Why your portfolio drifts even when you never touch it

Say you built a 70% stocks, 30% bonds portfolio in 2019 because that felt like the right risk level at the time. Stocks have a strong run over the next five years while bonds trade sideways. Without a single trade, that 70/30 split can quietly become 82/18. Nobody decided to take on more risk. The market decided for you.

This is the part most people miss about portfolio rebalancing: it's not about picking better investments. It's about periodically resetting the mix back to what you actually intended, so a good run in one asset class doesn't accidentally concentrate your risk. Left alone, winners keep growing as a share of the portfolio and losers keep shrinking, until the account no longer reflects the risk tolerance you started with.

## Your risk tolerance changes even if your portfolio doesn't

Risk tolerance investing isn't a one-time questionnaire you fill out when you open an account. It shifts with your life. A 38-year-old building a bar and grill with three years of runway left on a loan can absorb more volatility than a 58-year-old who just sold that same business and is living off the proceeds. A new rental property purchase changes your liquidity needs. A child heading to college in two years changes your time horizon.

The Federal Reserve's research on household finance and investment behavior points to something worth sitting with: household decisions around saving and investing often lag real changes in financial circumstances by years, not months (see the [Federal Reserve's Notes on Household Finance and Investment Behavior](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/default.htm)). People keep investing the way they did five years ago because that's the plan they set up, not because it still fits. A portfolio that made sense when you were building your business doesn't automatically make sense once the business is stable, sold, or handed to a partner.

## Rebalancing triggers a tax bill if you don't plan for it

Here's where a lot of DIY rebalancing goes wrong. Selling appreciated stock to bring an allocation back in line feels like housekeeping, but in a taxable brokerage account, it's a taxable event. Sell a position you've held for over a year and you owe long-term capital gains tax. Sell something held less than a year, and it's taxed as ordinary income, often a much bigger hit.

There are ways around this that most people never use because nobody tells them:

- Rebalance inside a 401(k) or IRA first, where trades don't trigger a current tax bill.
- Direct new contributions toward the underweighted asset class instead of selling the overweighted one.
- Pair gains with tax-loss harvesting elsewhere in the portfolio in the same calendar year.
- Time larger rebalancing trades around a lower-income year, if one is coming.

## Diversification and asset allocation solve different problems

People use portfolio diversification and asset allocation interchangeably, but they answer different questions. Asset allocation is the big-picture split, stocks versus bonds versus cash versus real estate. Diversification is what happens inside each of those buckets, so a downturn in one company, sector, or property doesn't take the whole portfolio down with it.

This gets more complicated for clients who already have real estate exposure through their business or personal holdings. If you own a rental property in Rye or Mamaroneck alongside a brokerage account, that property is part of your overall allocation, whether you think of it that way or not. A portfolio that looks diversified on a Schwab statement can still be overweighted in real estate once you count the rental income, the equity, and the mortgage risk sitting outside the brokerage entirely.

| Concept | What it controls | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Asset allocation | Overall mix of asset classes | 60% stocks, 30% bonds, 10% real estate |
| Diversification | Spread within an asset class | 15 stocks across 6 sectors instead of 3 tech stocks |

## Common Questions

**How often should I rebalance my portfolio?**
Most financial professionals suggest reviewing allocation at least once a year, or any time one asset class drifts more than 5 to 10 percentage points from its target. The right frequency depends on how volatile your holdings are and how much new money you're adding regularly.

**Does rebalancing always trigger capital gains tax?**
Only in taxable accounts. Rebalancing inside a 401(k), IRA, or Roth account doesn't create a current tax bill. In a taxable brokerage account, selling appreciated positions to rebalance does trigger gains, which is why timing and tax-loss harvesting matter.

**What's the real difference between diversification and asset allocation?**
Asset allocation is your split across major categories like stocks, bonds, cash, and real estate. Diversification is how spread out your holdings are within each of those categories. You can have a diversified stock portfolio that's still badly allocated if it's 90% of your net worth.

**Can a CPA help me rebalance, or do I need a financial advisor?**
Both roles matter, and they're not interchangeable. A financial advisor typically handles the trades and investment selection. A CPA handles the tax consequences of those trades and how they fit into your broader tax planning, business income, and year-end strategy.
