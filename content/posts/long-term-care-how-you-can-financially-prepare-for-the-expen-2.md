A phone call from a hospital discharge planner is not the moment to start thinking about how you'll pay for long-term care. By then, the choices are narrower and more expensive, and the family is making decisions under stress instead of with a plan. Long-term care is one of the few expenses in retirement that can run six figures a year, and it rarely gives much warning. Treating it as a financial-risk problem, something you fund years ahead through insurance, benefits, and savings, puts you in a completely different position than treating it as a bill that shows up when a parent falls or a diagnosis changes everything.

Most people underestimate two things at once: how much care actually costs, and how many funding tools exist beyond "pay out of pocket." Closing that gap is the job of a financial plan, not a last-minute scramble.

## Why the price tag surprises most families

In Westchester County and the surrounding areas we serve, the cost of care has climbed well past what people remember from their parents' generation. A home health aide working part-time can run several thousand dollars a month. Assisted living communities in the New York metro area frequently charge $6,000 to $8,000 a month depending on the level of care. A private room in a skilled nursing facility can exceed $150,000 a year in this region, and Medicare covers almost none of it beyond a short post-hospital stay.

Here's a rough sense of what different levels of care can cost annually in the lower Hudson Valley and greater New York area:

| Type of care | Approximate annual cost |
|---|---|
| In-home aide, part-time (20 hrs/week) | $35,000 - $50,000 |
| In-home aide, full-time | $80,000 - $110,000 |
| Assisted living facility | $70,000 - $95,000 |
| Private room, nursing home | $150,000 - $180,000 |

Those ranges move with wage inflation and staffing shortages every year, which is exactly why a plan built five or ten years out needs to assume costs will be higher than today's numbers, not the same.

## Three ways to fund care, and why most families need all three

No single source usually covers the full cost. The families who handle this well tend to blend three tools: insurance, veterans benefits where applicable, and personal savings earmarked specifically for care.

Long-term care insurance transfers the risk to an insurer in exchange for a premium, paid either as a standalone policy or bundled into a hybrid life insurance or annuity product. Veterans Aid and Attendance is a benefit that gets overlooked constantly, even though it can pay eligible wartime veterans and surviving spouses a meaningful monthly amount toward the cost of care. And self-funding, using savings, home equity, or investment income, works for some households, but only if the amount set aside is realistic against the cost table above, not a guess.

## Long-term care insurance vs. self-funding: the real tradeoffs

Insurance makes the most sense earlier in life, generally in your 50s or early 60s, when premiums are lower and health underwriting is more forgiving. A policy purchased at 55 might carry a premium a third or less of what the same coverage would cost at 65, assuming you still qualify at all. The tradeoff is that premiums are ongoing, and if you never need care, the money spent on premiums doesn't come back the way self-funded savings would.

Self-funding avoids the ongoing premium and keeps money flexible if it's never needed for care. The tradeoff is concentration risk: if a nursing home stay runs three or four years at $160,000 a year, that's $500,000 or more coming out of a single household's assets, often at the same time a surviving spouse still needs income to live on. Families who self-fund successfully tend to run the numbers with a CPA first, stress-testing what happens to the rest of the household's retirement plan if care lasts longer than expected.

A hybrid approach is common: a modest long-term care insurance policy or hybrid life/LTC product to cap the worst-case cost, combined with a dedicated savings bucket for the gap. Some of these costs may also be deductible as qualified medical expenses if they exceed the AGI threshold for itemized deductions, per [IRS Publication 502](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p502), which is worth factoring into the plan rather than discovering at tax time.

## Veterans Aid and Attendance, a benefit worth checking early

For households with a wartime veteran or a surviving spouse, the VA's Aid and Attendance benefit can offset a real portion of in-home care, assisted living, or nursing home costs. Eligibility depends on service history, income, and net worth limits, and the application process takes time, often several months. Waiting until care is already underway to apply means paying full price out of pocket while the claim works its way through. Checking eligibility years ahead, even before care is needed, means the paperwork is ready to move the moment it's necessary. Full details on eligibility and the current benefit structure are available directly from the [VA's Aid and Attendance and Housebound benefits page](https://www.va.gov/pension/aid-attendance-housebound/).

## Fitting the plan into your broader tax and retirement picture

Long-term care funding doesn't sit in isolation from the rest of a household's finances. Pulling from a traditional IRA to pay for care creates taxable income in the same year, which can push someone into a higher bracket or trigger higher Medicare premiums the following year through IRMAA surcharges. Timing withdrawals, coordinating them with other income sources, and knowing which medical expenses are deductible under IRS Publication 502 all change the after-tax cost of a care plan by thousands of dollars a year.

## Common Questions

**How much does long-term care actually cost in the New York area?**
In Westchester County and the greater New York metro area, a private room in a nursing home commonly runs $150,000 to $180,000 a year, assisted living runs $70,000 to $95,000 a year, and a full-time home health aide can cost $80,000 to $110,000 a year. Costs vary by facility and level of care, and they tend to rise faster than general inflation.

**Is long-term care insurance worth it if I'm healthy now?**
For many people in their 50s, yes. Premiums are lower and underwriting is easier before health conditions develop, and a policy purchased early can cap what would otherwise be an open-ended expense later. The right answer depends on family health history, existing savings, and whether a hybrid life/LTC product or standalone policy fits your budget better.

**Can I get VA benefits and still use my own savings for care?**
Yes. VA Aid and Attendance is designed to supplement other funding sources, not replace them. Most families combine the VA benefit with personal savings, and some also carry a long-term care insurance policy to cover costs above what the VA benefit and savings can handle together.

**Are long-term care expenses tax deductible?**
Some qualified long-term care expenses can be deducted as medical expenses if your total unreimbursed medical costs exceed the applicable percentage of your adjusted gross income, as outlined in [IRS Publication 502](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p502). Whether a specific facility or in-home care arrangement qualifies depends on the details, which is why it's worth reviewing with a CPA before assuming a deduction applies.
