Insurance conversations usually start backward. Someone asks what policies they should buy, and the honest answer is: it depends on what you'd lose if things went wrong, not what a broker has on a rate sheet this month. Buying a policy because your contractor friend has one, or adding an umbrella because someone mentioned it at a networking event, isn't a strategy. It's a guess dressed up as prudence.

The better question is what would actually hurt you financially, how likely is that event, and what's the cheapest way to transfer that risk instead of carrying it yourself. That's a calculation tied to your net worth, your cash flow, and the specific exposures in your business or household. It isn't a checklist copied from a template, and it isn't a list of every insurance product that exists.

Stephen P. Pryor, CPA, Shawn Wilson, CPA, and Denise Romano, CPA aren't licensed insurance brokers, and this isn't a pitch to become one. What the firm does instead is sit with a client's actual financials, entity structure, and cash position and figure out where the real gaps sit before that client ever calls a broker. That conversation tends to save money two ways: it stops people from over-insuring low-probability items out of anxiety, and it catches the one exposure, usually liability or income interruption, that would actually be catastrophic.

## What insurance is really protecting

Insurance doesn't protect stuff. It protects net worth and future earning power. A $6,000 kitchen renovation isn't what an umbrella policy exists for. A slip-and-fall lawsuit against a bar owner with $900,000 in home equity and a rental property is exactly what it exists for.

Here's a case that comes up often in Port Chester and Westchester County construction work: a contractor carries $500,000 in general liability, has $380,000 in business equipment, and personally guarantees a truck lease. If a subcontractor's employee is injured on a jobsite and the claim runs past that $500,000 limit, the excess doesn't disappear. It becomes a claim against the contractor's personal assets, including a house that may have $400,000 in equity. That's the gap an umbrella policy is built to close, and it usually costs $300 to $500 a year for the first $1 million in coverage. Compare that to the premium some owners pay for a low-deductible property policy that would only ever pay out a few thousand dollars, and the money is clearly going to the wrong place.

The same logic applies to individuals. A $50 deductible on a homeowner's policy sounds protective, but it adds hundreds of dollars a year in premium to avoid a cost most households could cover out of a checking account. Meanwhile, a self-employed 42-year-old with no disability insurance is exposed to something far bigger: losing income for a year with nothing to replace it. Coverage decisions should follow severity and probability, not comfort.

## Personal coverage comes down to three numbers

For individuals and households, the analysis usually reduces to three figures.

First, income replacement. If you have dependents or a mortgage, the standard planning range is 8 to 12 times annual income in term life coverage, adjusted for existing savings and debt. A 45-year-old earning $150,000 with a $400,000 mortgage balance and two kids needs a materially different policy than a 55-year-old with the mortgage paid off and grown children.

Second, the liability gap. Take your net worth, including home equity, investment accounts, and business interests, and subtract the liability limits on your auto and homeowner's policies. Whatever's left is exposed. That's the number an umbrella policy is sized to cover, and for most households in the $500,000 to $2 million net worth range, $1 million to $2 million in umbrella coverage closes it affordably.

Third, disability income. Social Security disability rarely replaces more than a fraction of pre-disability earnings, and the approval process can take a year or more. For a self-employed individual or a small business owner drawing a salary, an individual disability policy replacing 60% of income is often the most underfunded piece of the entire plan, and the one people skip because it feels less urgent than life insurance. ## Construction, real estate, and bars carry different exposure profiles

Business coverage should be sized to how the business actually operates, not to an industry-generic list. A landlord's risk profile has almost nothing in common with a bar owner's, even though both might buy a policy labeled "general liability."

| Industry | Highest-severity exposure | Coverage that usually matters most | Coverage that's often overbought |
|---|---|---|
| Construction | Jobsite injury, subcontractor liability, equipment loss | Workers' comp with a managed EMR, commercial umbrella, builder's risk | Low-deductible tool and equipment riders |
| Real estate | Tenant injury, property damage, loss of rental income | Landlord liability, business interruption/loss of rents, umbrella | Redundant coverage across multiple small policies per property |
| Bars and restaurants | Liquor liability, cash handling exposure, kitchen fire | Liquor liability ($1M/$2M limits are standard in New York), property with replacement cost valuation, business interruption | Extended warranties on kitchen equipment |

A construction company's workers' compensation experience modification rate (EMR) drives premium more than almost anything else on the policy, and it's tied directly to how claims are managed and how payroll is classified, which is exactly the kind of detail that shows up in clean, current books. Our [construction accounting page](/industries/construction) covers how job costing and payroll classification connect to that number.

For real estate owners, the bigger risk is often underinsured loss of rents, not property damage itself. If a fire takes a four-unit building offline for eight months, the mortgage doesn't pause. Business interruption coverage sized to actual rent roll, not a rough estimate, is what keeps that gap from becoming a cash flow crisis. More on how that connects to depreciation and entity structure is on our [real estate industry page](/industries/real-estate).

For bars and restaurants, liquor liability is the exposure most owners underestimate until there's a claim, and it's frequently excluded from a standard general liability policy entirely, requiring a separate endorsement. Given New York's dram shop exposure, that's not a place to shop for the cheapest option. Our [restaurant industry page](/industries/restaurants) goes into the broader financial picture, including cash handling and tip reporting, that tends to surface alongside this conversation.

On the tax side, business insurance premiums are generally deductible as ordinary and necessary business expenses, which the IRS outlines in [Publication 535](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p535). That deductibility doesn't change whether a policy is the right size, but it does mean the after-tax cost of adequate coverage is usually lower than owners assume when they're deciding whether to bump up a limit.

## Running your own insurance needs analysis

This doesn't require a broker meeting to start. A rough pass looks like this:

1. List total net worth: cash, investments, home equity, business equity.
2. List annual income that would need replacing if you or a co-owner couldn't work.
3. List your current liability limits across auto, home, and business policies.
4. Subtract step 3 from step 1. That gap is your umbrella or excess liability need.
5. For a business, list the single event that would cost the most: a jobsite injury, a tenant lawsuit, a liquor liability claim, a fire that closes the kitchen for six months. Price out what that event would actually cost in lost income and legal exposure, then compare it to current limits.

The numbers behind steps 1 through 3 are only as good as the bookkeeping and financial data behind them, which is part of why this conversation tends to happen naturally during a firm's year-round advisory work rather than as a once-a-year checklist. When the firm has real-time visibility into a client's cash position and entity structure through its secure online platform, sizing these numbers takes a working session, not weeks of document gathering.

## Common Questions

**How much insurance coverage do I actually need?**
There's no flat dollar figure that applies to everyone. The right amount is whatever closes the gap between your net worth and your current liability limits, plus enough income replacement and disability coverage to protect your household if you couldn't work. For most people with a home and $500,000 or more in combined assets, that means an umbrella policy of at least $1 million on top of standard auto and homeowner's limits.

**What's the difference between insuring my business for what I own versus what I could lose?**
Insuring for what you own means covering the replacement cost of equipment, inventory, or property. Insuring for what you could lose means covering the financial consequence of a lawsuit, an injury claim, or months of lost income, which is almost always a bigger number. Most underinsurance problems show up in the second category, not the first.

**Is business insurance tax deductible?**
Generally, yes. Premiums for insurance that's ordinary and necessary for running your business, including liability, property, and workers' compensation coverage, are deductible business expenses under IRS guidance in [Publication 535](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p535). Coverage decisions should still be based on risk exposure first, since deductibility only affects the after-tax cost, not whether the coverage is the right size.

**How often should I revisit my coverage limits?**
At minimum, any time your net worth changes materially, you add a property, your payroll grows, or you open a new location. For most construction, real estate, and hospitality businesses, an annual review alongside tax planning catches the changes that matter most, since revenue growth and asset purchases are often the same events that widen a liability gap.
