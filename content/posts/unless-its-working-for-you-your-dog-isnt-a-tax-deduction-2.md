Every year we get some version of the same question, usually from a construction client, a landlord, or a bar owner: can I write off my dog? The honest answer is almost never, and the exceptions are narrower than most of what shows up in a quick internet search. The IRS doesn't care that your dog patrols the lumber yard at night or greets regulars at the bar. It cares whether the dog performs verifiable, ordinary, and necessary work for a business, or meets a documented medical need. Get that distinction right and a few real deductions open up. Get it wrong and you've built part of your return on a position that won't hold up if it's ever questioned.

## Why your family dog almost never qualifies

Personal expenses stay personal, no matter how useful the dog feels. Food, grooming, vet visits, boarding: the average dog owner spends somewhere between $1,000 and $2,000 a year on these costs, and none of it is deductible just because the dog rides along in the work truck or naps under your desk at the office. The IRS standard for a business expense, laid out in [IRS Publication 535](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p535), is that it has to be ordinary and necessary for running the business. Companionship, morale, or making the shop feel more welcoming doesn't meet that bar, even if it's true.

This is the mistake we see most often with construction and property owners who keep a dog on site. The dog might genuinely deter trespassers, but if there's no business purpose documented beyond "he's just always there," the deduction won't survive a second look.

## Service and guide dogs are a real medical deduction, with real rules

This one is legitimate, and it lives on the individual return, not the business return. [IRS Publication 502](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p502) allows a medical expense deduction for the cost of buying, training, and maintaining a guide dog or other service animal used by someone with a visual impairment, hearing impairment, or other physical disability. That includes food, grooming, and veterinary care tied to keeping the animal able to perform its task. It's claimed as an itemized medical expense on Schedule A, subject to the 7.5% adjusted gross income floor, which means it only helps once your total medical costs clear that threshold.

Therapy dogs are a different story, and this is where the therapy dog deduction IRS question usually falls apart. A dog that provides general comfort or emotional support, without being trained to perform a specific task tied to a diagnosed disability, generally doesn't meet the IRS definition of a service animal. Visiting hospitals or nursing homes with a friendly dog is a wonderful thing to do, but it doesn't create a medical deduction on its own. ## Guard dogs can be a legitimate business expense

Here's where the guard dog business expense question gets interesting, because this one can actually work. A dog kept specifically to secure a construction yard, storage lot, or warehouse can qualify as an ordinary and necessary business expense under the same standard in Publication 535, but the facts have to support it. That means the breed fits the job (a German Shepherd or Rottweiler makes a stronger case than a Chihuahua), the dog is housed at the business location rather than the owner's home, and the food, vet care, and training costs are tracked separately from personal pet expenses.

We work with contractors across Port Chester and the tri-state area who keep equipment and materials on unattended job sites overnight, and a documented guard dog can be a reasonable line item on the business return. The key word is documented. If the same dog also lives in the house and rides in the family car on weekends, that mixed use is exactly what draws questions. Our [construction](/industries/construction) clients who get this right keep a simple log: where the dog is kept, what it guards, and receipts that are billed to the business, not the owner personally.

## Breeding dogs is a business only if you run it like one

A breeding operation can be a legitimate business, with real deductions for stud fees, veterinary bills, advertising, and supplies against the income from selling puppies. But the IRS applies hobby loss rules under Internal Revenue Code Section 183, and it looks at whether you're actually operating for profit: separate business records, a business bank account, marketing effort, and a track record of trying to make money rather than just occasionally selling a litter. If the activity doesn't meet that profit-motive test, the income is still taxable, but the expenses aren't deductible against it, which is the worst version of this outcome.

If breeding is more than a side interest for you, it's worth setting it up properly from the start. That usually means choosing the right entity and keeping the books separate from day one, which is exactly the kind of decision our [entity type analysis](/services/advisory/entity-type-analysis) work is built for.

## Where the different categories land

| Type of dog | Deductible? | Where it's claimed | What you need to document |
|---|---|---|---|
| Family pet, even one that "guards" the house | No | Not applicable | Not applicable |
| Certified service or guide dog | Yes, as a medical expense | Schedule A, subject to the 7.5% AGI floor | Documented disability, cost of the dog, training, food, and vet care |
| Therapy or emotional support dog without a trained task | Usually no | Not applicable | Rarely qualifies without a diagnosed condition and a specific trained task |
| Guard dog kept at a business location | Yes, as a business expense | Schedule C or the business return | Breed suited to security work, kept on-site, costs billed to the business |
| Breeding dog run for profit | Yes, if operated as a business | Schedule C or the business return | Business records, marketing, sales history, profit motive under Section 183 |

## Keep the paperwork or the deduction won't survive

Every category above depends on documentation, not intention. A doctor's note and a training record support the service dog deduction. A log of where the guard dog is housed and receipts billed to the business support the guard dog expense. Litter sale records, a business bank account, and evidence of marketing support the breeding deduction. Without that paper trail, even a legitimate write-off looks like a guess if a return ever gets a second look.

This is really a bookkeeping problem as much as a tax one. If dog-related costs are mixed in with personal spending on the same credit card, nobody can reconstruct what was business and what wasn't by April. Clients who run these expenses through a dedicated business account, reconciled monthly, have a much easier time defending the deduction than clients trying to piece it together at tax time. ## Common Questions

**Can I deduct my dog's food and vet bills if she comes to work with me every day?**
No, not just for showing up. The IRS looks for a specific business function, like documented security work at a job site or storage yard, not general presence at the workplace. Without a defined role and separate records, food and vet costs stay personal, even if the dog is technically on the clock.

**Is a therapy dog tax deductible?**
Usually not. The medical deduction under IRS Publication 502 is built around service animals trained to perform a specific task for a diagnosed disability, not dogs that provide general comfort or visit facilities to lift people's spirits. Without that trained-task connection, the costs don't qualify as a medical expense.

**Can I write off a guard dog for my construction business?**
Yes, if the facts support it. The dog should be a breed suited to security work, housed at the job site or yard rather than your home, and the related costs tracked separately from personal pet expenses. Mixed personal and business use is the most common reason this deduction gets challenged.

**What if I breed dogs on the side, is that ever deductible?**
It can be, if the activity is run for profit under Section 183 rather than as an occasional hobby. That means a business bank account, marketing, and a genuine effort to turn a profit over time. Without that structure, the income is still taxable, but the expenses aren't deductible against it.
