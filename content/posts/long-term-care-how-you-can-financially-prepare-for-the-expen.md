A woman in Rye called our office last spring because her father's assisted living facility had just raised its monthly rate to $9,800. She wanted to know how her family was supposed to keep paying that for another five years. The honest answer is that most families never plan for this cost until it's already showing up on a bill, and by then the cheapest options are already off the table. Long-term care is one of the few expenses in life that is both highly likely and almost entirely predictable in shape, if not in timing, which makes it a planning problem, not a crisis to react to.

About 70% of people who turn 65 will need some form of long-term care during their lifetime, whether that's a home health aide three days a week or full-time nursing home care. In New York, a private room in a nursing home now runs somewhere between $170,000 and $190,000 a year depending on the county, and Westchester is on the higher end of that range. Home health aides in the region typically bill $30 to $35 an hour, which adds up fast once care moves from a few hours a week to around-the-clock coverage. Medicare does not cover custodial care, the kind that helps with bathing, dressing, and daily living, so families are left choosing between insurance, veterans benefits, spending down savings, or some combination of the three.

## Why waiting until you need care is the most expensive option

Long-term care insurance underwriting looks at your health history, and insurers price policies based on age at application. Someone who applies at 55 pays meaningfully less per year than someone who waits until 65, and someone with a recent diagnosis of diabetes, Parkinson's, or early cognitive decline may not qualify at all. The same logic applies to Medicaid planning: New York has a five-year lookback period on asset transfers, so moving money or property to children the year before you need a nursing home does not protect it. Families who start the conversation at 55 or 60, while everyone involved is still healthy and clear-headed, end up with more options and lower costs than families who start at 78 after a fall or a stroke forces the issue.

## The three main ways to pay for long-term care

Most people [fund long-term care through some blend of three sources](/resources/long-term-care-how-you-can-financially-prepare-for-the-expen-2): private insurance, veterans benefits if they qualify, and personal savings or investments. None of these is automatically the right answer. The right mix depends on your health, your assets, your family's risk tolerance, and whether a parent or spouse served in the military.

| Funding source | Best fit for | Typical trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| Long-term care insurance | Healthy applicants in their 50s or early 60s | Premiums can rise over time; underwriting can deny coverage after a diagnosis |
| VA Aid and Attendance benefit | Wartime veterans and surviving spouses who need help with daily activities | Requires meeting service, income, and net worth thresholds |
| Self-funding through savings and investments | Households with substantial liquid assets or home equity | No coverage cap, but a multi-year stay can draw down a retirement portfolio quickly |

### Long-term care insurance versus self-funding

A traditional long-term care policy purchased at 58 might run $2,500 to $4,000 a year in premium and provide $150,000 to $200,000 in lifetime benefits, adjusted for inflation. That premium is a known, budgetable expense. Self-funding means setting aside enough in accessible investments to cover two to four years of care without that money doing anything else for your retirement. Some clients split the difference: they buy a smaller policy to cover the first two years of care, then plan to self-fund anything beyond that. The right split shows up clearly once we model your actual retirement income, Social Security timing, and portfolio, which is part of why long-term care planning works best alongside the [tax and retirement planning](/resources/e-books/maximizing-your-tax-refund-a-strategic-guide-to-tax-planning) you're likely already doing.

## The VA Aid and Attendance benefit most veterans don't know they qualify for

If you or your spouse served during a wartime period, the [VA Aid and Attendance benefit](https://www.va.gov/pension/aid-attendance-housebound/) can add a meaningful monthly payment on top of a standard VA pension, specifically to help cover the cost of in-home care, assisted living, or a nursing home. Eligibility depends on service dates, medical need for assistance with daily activities like bathing or dressing, and income and asset limits that account for unreimbursed medical expenses. We've walked several Westchester County families through this application, and the paperwork is detailed enough that it's worth having someone check your numbers before you file, since a denial can set a family back months while care costs keep accruing.

## Where the tax code helps and where it doesn't

Qualified long-term care insurance premiums are deductible as medical expenses, subject to age-based limits that increase as you get older, and unreimbursed long-term care costs themselves may also count toward the medical expense deduction once you clear the 7.5% of adjusted gross income threshold. [IRS Publication 502](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p502) lays out exactly which long-term care premiums and services qualify. That deduction won't offset the full cost of care, but for a family paying $170,000 a year out of pocket, it's not nothing, and it's easy to miss if your tax preparer doesn't know to ask about it.

## Common Questions

**How much does long-term care actually cost in Westchester County?**
A private room in a Westchester County nursing home typically runs $170,000 to $190,000 a year. Home health aides usually bill $30 to $35 an hour, and assisted living facilities in the area often start around $7,000 to $10,000 a month, with costs rising as care needs increase.

**Is long-term care insurance worth it if I'm already in my early 60s?**
It depends on your health and assets. Premiums are higher than they'd be at 55, but a policy bought at 62 or 63 can still be cheaper than self-funding two or three years of nursing home care outright. The math changes based on your specific health history, which is why it's worth running the numbers before assuming you've missed the window.

**Can I get VA benefits and still use my personal savings for care?**
Yes. The VA Aid and Attendance benefit is designed to supplement other funding sources, not replace them. Most families who qualify use it alongside savings, a pension, or an insurance payout to cover the full cost of care.

**Are long-term care insurance premiums tax deductible?**
Qualified long-term care insurance premiums are deductible as medical expenses up to age-based limits set by the IRS, and they count toward the same 7.5% of adjusted gross income threshold as other medical costs. [IRS Publication 502](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p502) has the current limits by age.
