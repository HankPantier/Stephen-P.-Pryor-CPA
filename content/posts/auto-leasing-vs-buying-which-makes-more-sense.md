A contractor in Port Chester walks into a dealership needing a new work truck, and within ten minutes the salesperson has three lease offers on the table and zero mention of what happens to his tax return in April. That's the problem with most lease-vs-buy advice: it starts and ends with the monthly payment. The better question is what each option does to your cash flow, your tax bill, and your balance sheet over the next three to five years.

This decision shows up constantly for our clients in construction, real estate, and hospitality. A general contractor needs a truck that can survive job sites. A real estate agent puts 25,000 miles a year on a car driving clients to showings across Westchester County. A pub owner needs a reliable vehicle for supply runs and bank deposits. Each of these situations can point toward a different answer, and the right one rarely matches what the finance manager recommends.

## Start with cash flow, not the monthly payment

Leasing almost always wins the sticker-price comparison. Lower monthly payments, little or no money down, and you're driving a newer vehicle every three years. For a business watching cash flow closely, that lower payment can matter more than the long-term math. A bar owner managing thin margins and unpredictable weekly revenue may need that lower fixed cost more than the eventual equity a purchased vehicle builds.

Buying costs more upfront and month to month, but you're building an asset. Once the loan is paid off, the payment disappears and the vehicle still has value. For a construction business that keeps trucks running for eight or ten years past the loan term, that math flips in favor of buying. The decision isn't about which option is cheaper in a vacuum. It's about which cash flow pattern fits how your business actually runs.

## What leasing gets you, and what it quietly costs

Leasing works well for predictable, moderate mileage and a preference for newer vehicles with fewer repair costs. Most leases run 36 months with mileage caps between 10,000 and 15,000 miles a year, and going over that cap triggers per-mile penalties that add up fast for a real estate agent covering multiple towns in a single day.

The quiet cost is that you never build equity. At the end of the lease, you hand back the keys and start over, payment and all. If your vehicle takes a beating, whether that's a contractor hauling tools or a landscaper towing a trailer, wear-and-tear charges at lease-end can turn a good deal into an expensive one.

## What buying gets you, and where it gets expensive

Buying gives you an asset with no mileage restrictions, no wear-and-tear inspection, and the freedom to keep the vehicle as long as it runs. Once it's paid off, your only ongoing cost is maintenance and insurance, which is why business owners who put real miles on a vehicle, real estate agents especially, often come out ahead over five or six years.

The expensive part is depreciation and repair risk sitting entirely on you. A vehicle loses much of its value in the first three years whether you're driving it or not, and once the warranty ends, repair bills land on your books instead of the leasing company's.

## The tax rules shift once the vehicle works for your business

Here's where the decision stops being a personal finance question and becomes a tax planning one. If a vehicle is used for business, both leasing and buying create deductions, but they work differently.

With a lease, you generally deduct the business-use percentage of your monthly lease payment as an operating expense, year after year, for as long as you lease. With a purchase, you can potentially deduct a large portion of the vehicle's cost in the first year through Section 179 expensing and bonus depreciation, following the guidance in [IRS Publication 946](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p946).

The catch is that vehicle type matters. Passenger cars are subject to annual luxury auto depreciation limits, which cap how much you can write off each year regardless of the purchase price. Trucks and SUVs with a gross vehicle weight rating over 6,000 pounds are treated differently and can qualify for a much larger first-year Section 179 deduction, often in the range of $30,500 for the 2024 tax year, subject to the limits detailed in IRS Publication 946. That distinction alone has pushed more than one contractor client toward buying a heavy-duty truck instead of leasing a lighter one.

Interest rates matter here too. Auto loan rates have moved with the broader credit market, and the Federal Reserve's [Consumer Credit (G.19) report](https://www.federalreserve.gov/releases/g19/current/) tracks those trends directly. A higher rate environment narrows the gap between leasing and buying, since financing a purchase becomes more expensive relative to a lease payment that's already baked into the manufacturer's terms.

## Lease vs. buy, side by side

| Factor | Leasing | Buying |
|---|---|---|
| Upfront cost | Lower, often little down | Higher down payment or full price |
| Monthly payment | Typically lower | Typically higher |
| Mileage | Capped, penalties apply over limit | Unlimited |
| Long-term ownership | None, vehicle returned at lease-end | Full equity once paid off |
| Repair risk | Limited, mostly under warranty | Falls to owner after warranty ends |
| Tax treatment | Deduct business-use percentage of payments | Section 179 and depreciation, subject to IRS limits |
| Best fit | Lower annual mileage, frequent upgrades, tight cash flow | High mileage, long hold periods, heavier vehicles |

## Which path fits your situation

A construction business running heavy trucks that log 30,000 miles a year on job sites across the Hudson Valley usually comes out ahead buying, both for the mileage freedom and the Section 179 treatment available on qualifying vehicles. You can see how this kind of first-year depreciation strategy plays out for equipment and vehicles alike in our piece on [cost segregation studies for construction companies](/resources/cost-segregation-studies-are-a-construction-companys-most-ov-2).

A real estate agent who drives clients to two or three showings a day but replaces vehicles every few years for image and reliability may prefer leasing, especially if mileage stays under the cap. Landlords and investors juggling other depreciation decisions on their properties should look at how vehicle deductions interact with the rest of their tax planning; our guide on [real estate CPA services](/industries/real-estate) covers how those pieces fit together.

A bar or pub owner using a vehicle mainly for supply runs and bank deposits, with modest annual mileage, often finds leasing the simpler, lower-stress choice, freeing up cash for inventory and payroll instead.

The honest answer is that neither option is universally better. It depends on how many miles you drive, how long you keep vehicles, what type of vehicle you need, and how your business handles cash flow month to month. That's a conversation worth having with a CPA before you sign anything, not after.

## Common Questions

**Is it better to lease or buy a car for tax purposes?**
It depends on the vehicle and how it's used. Leasing lets you deduct the business-use portion of your monthly payment every year. Buying can allow a larger first-year deduction through Section 179 and bonus depreciation, particularly for trucks and SUVs over 6,000 pounds gross vehicle weight, as outlined in [IRS Publication 946](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p946). Passenger cars face annual depreciation caps regardless of which option you choose.

**Can I deduct a car lease as a business expense?**
Yes, if the vehicle is used for business. You deduct the business-use percentage of your lease payment, along with related costs like fuel, insurance, and maintenance tied to that same percentage. Keeping a mileage log is essential to support the deduction if the IRS asks.

**Does Section 179 apply to leased vehicles?**
No. Section 179 is a depreciation deduction, which only applies to vehicles you own. If you lease, you deduct the payments themselves as an operating expense instead, spread across the lease term rather than taken upfront.

**How many miles a year makes buying the better choice?**
There's no single number, but once you're consistently driving more than the 10,000 to 15,000 miles a year typical lease caps allow, the penalties for excess mileage start eating into the savings leasing offered in the first place. High-mileage drivers, including many real estate agents and contractors, tend to come out ahead buying.
