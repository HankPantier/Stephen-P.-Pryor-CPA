If you build homes for a living, percentage-of-completion accounting has probably cost you more sleep than any subcontractor ever has. You end up recognizing income before the customer's final draw comes in, the tax bill shows up ahead of the cash, and job costing turns into a moving target every time a change order comes through. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed into law in 2025, widened the home construction exception under IRC Section 460, and that means more residential builders and developers can now skip percentage-of-completion accounting entirely. For contracts entered into starting in 2026, the method you choose now could change how much cash stays in your business for years.

## The rule that pushes builders onto percentage of completion

Section 460 requires long-term contracts, meaning any contract that spans more than one tax year, to use the percentage-of-completion method (PCM). Under PCM, you recognize income as costs are incurred relative to total estimated contract cost, whether or not the customer has paid you yet. Two exceptions existed before OBBBA: home construction contracts (limited to buildings with four or fewer dwelling units) and a small contractor exception based on average annual gross receipts, which sat around $30 million after inflation adjustments. Everyone else, including a lot of mid-size home builders and multi-unit residential developers, got stuck estimating taxable income on houses that hadn't closed yet.

## OBBBA widens who qualifies for the exception

The new law raises that gross receipts threshold substantially, moving it from roughly $30 million to about $80 million in average annual gross receipts, and broadens which residential contracts fall under the home construction exception. That single change pulls a meaningful number of regional builders and developers out of mandatory PCM for the first time. The catch is timing: the expanded rule applies to contracts entered into after the law's enactment date, not to contracts already in progress. A calendar-year builder signing new construction contracts in 2026 is the taxpayer this rule was written for.

## Completed contract versus percentage of completion in real dollars

Say a builder signs an 18-month, $2 million spec home contract. Under PCM, if the home is 60 percent complete by year-end, roughly 60 percent of the projected gross profit gets taxed that year, even though the house hasn't sold and no final payment has arrived. Under the completed contract method, available now to more builders thanks to the expanded exception, all of that income and expense gets reported in the year the home actually closes. That's the year the cash shows up.

PCM contracts also carry a look-back requirement: when the final numbers come in different from your estimates, you may owe interest on the difference, calculated on [IRS Form 8697](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8697). Contracts that qualify for the home construction exception skip that calculation altogether, which removes a recurring compliance headache along with the cash flow timing issue.

| | Percentage of completion | Completed contract (under the exception) |
|---|---|---|
| When income is recognized | As costs are incurred, based on cost-to-cost estimates | When the contract is complete and the unit closes |
| Cash flow effect | Tax often due before cash is collected | Tax due closer to when cash is in hand |
| Look-back interest, Form 8697 | Required recalculation most years | Not required |
| Small contractor threshold | Must use PCM above the threshold | Roughly $80 million average annual gross receipts under OBBBA, up from about $30 million |

## Why 2026 contracts are the date that matters

Because the expanded exception applies to contracts entered into after enactment, a builder can end up running two accounting methods side by side: PCM on contracts signed in 2024 and 2025, and completed contract on everything signed starting in 2026. That's manageable if you plan for it and messy if you don't. Before signing new contracts next year, it's worth reviewing your backlog, your average gross receipts over the prior three years, and whether your projects still meet the dwelling-unit definition for the home construction exception. Waiting until your 2026 return is due is the expensive way to find out you qualified.

## Making the switch without tripping over Form 3115

Switching from PCM to completed contract, or adopting completed contract for new contracts while retaining PCM on older ones, is an accounting method change. That generally means filing Form 3115 with the IRS, and getting the timing right matters as much as getting the math right. Builders sometimes assume the switch happens automatically the moment they qualify under the new gross receipts threshold. It doesn't. The election has to be made, documented, and filed correctly, and a mistimed filing can push the benefit out an entire tax year. The [AICPA's tax resources](https://www.aicpa-cima.com/topic/tax) are a reasonable place to track ongoing guidance as the IRS issues procedures for implementing the OBBBA changes, but the filing itself should be handled with your actual contract and gross receipts numbers in front of you, not a general FAQ.

## Common Questions

**Does the expanded exception mean every residential builder can use completed contract accounting now?**
Not automatically. You still need to fall under the home construction definition or the small contractor gross receipts threshold, now roughly $80 million in average annual gross receipts under OBBBA. Multifamily developers and larger regional builders should confirm eligibility contract by contract rather than assume qualification.

**Do contracts I signed in 2024 or 2025 switch to the new rule automatically?**
No. The expanded exception applies to contracts entered into after the law's enactment date. Contracts already in progress stay on whatever method you originally elected, which means some builders will run two methods side by side for a few years.

**What happens if I miss the window to file a method change before signing 2026 contracts?**
You can still file Form 3115 to change methods, but a late or mistimed filing can push the cash flow benefit out an entire tax year. Reviewing your contract pipeline and gross receipts history before year-end gives you time to file correctly instead of retroactively.

**Does switching methods eliminate the Form 8697 look-back calculation?**
Yes, for contracts that qualify under the home construction exception and use completed contract accounting, the look-back interest recalculation required under [Form 8697 instructions](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8697) no longer applies to those contracts.
