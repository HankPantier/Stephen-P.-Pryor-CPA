A construction company in Port Chester puts up a new office and equipment yard for $1.8 million, pays the tax bill on schedule, and depreciates the whole thing over 39 years like it's one giant asset. Across town, a real estate investor buys a $1.8 million apartment building, runs a cost segregation study, and writes off a third of that cost in year one. Same tax code, wildly different outcomes. The construction company usually has more to gain from that study than the investor does, and most owners never ask about it.

That's the gap worth closing. Cost segregation gets marketed as a real estate investor's tool that construction companies happen to qualify for. It actually fits construction better than almost any other industry, for reasons that have nothing to do with buying rental property and everything to do with how contractors already operate.

## Cost segregation breaks a building into pieces the tax code treats differently

Standard depreciation treats a commercial building as one asset, spread evenly over 39 years (27.5 for residential rental). A $2 million building gets roughly $51,000 a year in deductions, whether it has a warehouse, an office wing, a fenced equipment yard, or all three.

A cost segregation study, done by an engineer trained in this specific analysis, breaks that same building into its components. Electrical wiring dedicated to equipment, specialty plumbing, certain flooring systems, parking lots, fencing, and site improvements often qualify as 5-year, 7-year, or 15-year property instead of 39-year property. Nothing about the building changes. What changes is how fast you're allowed to deduct it.

That reclassification is the whole mechanism. Move 25 to 35 percent of a building's cost into shorter recovery periods, and you've moved a meaningful chunk of your tax deduction from year 30 to year one.

## Construction companies build the exact assets that make this work

A services firm that leases its office space has nothing to study. A construction company is different. Equipment yards, fabrication shops, fleet garages, spec buildings, build-to-suit projects, additions to an existing shop: these are assets construction businesses acquire and improve on a regular basis, not once every decade.

Add to that a structural problem specific to this industry: construction companies often run high effective tax rates because they're payroll-heavy, cash is tied up in equipment and materials, and much of that cash never shows up as a deduction the way a big depreciation write-off does. A company sitting on $3 million of self-owned facilities and equipment, paying tax on solid margins, is exactly the profile a cost segregation study is built for. If your company owns the ground it works from, that building is worth a second look. ## Self-performed work creates documentation most companies would have to pay to reconstruct

Here's the part that's easy to miss. Cost segregation studies need detailed cost documentation broken down by trade and building system, electrical separate from plumbing separate from sitework, to hold up if the IRS asks questions. Most businesses don't have that. They have a lump-sum invoice from a general contractor and have to pay an engineering firm to reverse-engineer the breakdown from blueprints, which drives up the cost of the study and slows it down.

A construction company that self-performs electrical, plumbing, HVAC, or sitework on its own facility already has this data. Job costing records that track labor and materials by phase and trade are, in effect, a rough draft of the cost segregation study. That's not a small advantage. It means the study is cheaper to run, faster to complete, and better documented if it's ever questioned. If your bookkeeping already separates costs by job and trade, you're most of the way there before an engineer even walks the site. This is one reason we tie [bookkeeping](/services/bookkeeping) so closely to tax strategy for our construction clients rather than treating them as separate conversations.

## Bonus depreciation timing changes the size of the payoff every year

Cost segregation and bonus depreciation work together. Once a component is reclassified into a shorter recovery period, it may also qualify for bonus depreciation in the year it's placed in service, which lets you deduct a large percentage of that reclassified cost immediately instead of spreading it over five or seven years.

The bonus depreciation percentage has moved year to year in recent tax law, and it can move again. That means the value of running a cost segregation study this year versus next year isn't fixed. A building placed in service in a year with a higher bonus depreciation rate can produce a dramatically larger first-year deduction than the identical building placed in service twelve months later. If you're planning a new shop, yard expansion, or spec build, the placed-in-service date deserves a real conversation before ground gets broken, not after the final invoice is paid. This kind of timing decision is a good fit for year-round [tax planning](/resources/e-books/maximizing-your-tax-refund-a-strategic-guide-to-tax-planning) rather than a once-a-year filing conversation.

## What a study actually finds, in real numbers

Here's a simplified example based on a $2.5 million self-built headquarters and equipment yard, the kind of project a mid-sized general contractor or specialty trade firm might complete for its own use.

| | Standard depreciation | After cost segregation |
|---|---|---|
| Recovery period | 39 years, straight-line | Split into 5, 7, 15, and 39-year components |
| Reclassified cost | $0 | Roughly $700,000 to $850,000 |
| Year-one deduction | About $64,000 | Can exceed $500,000, depending on the bonus depreciation rate in effect |
| Tax impact | Deduction spread evenly for decades | Deduction concentrated in early years, when cash flow often matters most |

The numbers shift based on the building's use, the components involved, and the tax rules in the year it's placed in service. ## Common Questions

**Does cost segregation only apply to buildings someone else constructed?**
No. It applies to any commercial building you own, whether you bought it, built it, or self-performed the construction yourself. Self-built facilities are often better candidates because detailed cost records already exist from the job costing process.

**Is there a minimum building size or cost for a cost segregation study to make sense?**
Most CPAs look for buildings costing $500,000 or more, since the study's cost needs to be worthwhile against the deductions it generates. Above that threshold, it's worth running the numbers regardless of the specific dollar figure.

**Does a cost segregation study increase audit risk?**
A properly documented study, prepared with engineering-based cost analysis and supported by job costing records, is defensible and commonly used. Sloppy or unsupported studies are the actual risk, which is why the underlying documentation matters as much as the deduction.

**What happens if I sell the building later?**
Accelerated depreciation can trigger depreciation recapture on sale, taxed at a different rate than the original deduction. This isn't a reason to avoid cost segregation, but it is a reason to plan the sale and the study together rather than treating them as separate decisions.
