A client asked us last month if she could write off her Bernese Mountain Dog because he "greets customers at the front desk" of her real estate office three days a week. It's a fair question, and it's one we hear more often than you'd think, especially from business owners in Port Chester and Westchester County who genuinely believe their dog earns its keep. The honest answer is almost always no. But almost never means never, and there are a handful of narrow, well-documented situations where dog-related costs are legitimately deductible under IRS rules. Knowing the difference matters, because getting it wrong on a return can trigger exactly the kind of scrutiny nobody wants.

## Why your golden retriever doesn't count as a write-off

The IRS treats pets as personal expenses, full stop. Food, grooming, vet visits, boarding, that adorable sweater for winter walks: none of it is deductible just because you own a business or work from home. The dog sitting under your desk during Zoom calls doesn't change the math. Personal expenses stay personal even when they happen to occur near your business activity, and "my dog makes me less stressed so I can work better" isn't a standard the IRS recognizes.

Where people get tripped up is assuming that any expense connected to daily business life automatically qualifies. It doesn't work that way for a home office plant, a gym membership, or a dog. The test under [IRS Publication 535](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p535) is whether an expense is ordinary and necessary for carrying on a trade or business. A comfort animal in a real estate office, however sweet, generally fails that test because the business would operate the same without the dog. That's the line the rest of this piece is drawn around.

## When a guard dog is a legitimate business expense

This is where it gets interesting for the construction and real estate clients we work with. If you own an active job site, a lumber yard, equipment storage lot, or vacant property under development, a trained guard dog protecting that property can be a deductible business expense. The costs that typically qualify include food, veterinary care, training, and even a portion of the dog's purchase price, usually depreciated as a business asset rather than expensed all at once.

The catch is that the dog actually has to do the job. A friendly lab that rides in the truck doesn't meet the bar. What holds up is a breed suited to security work (German Shepherds and Rottweilers show up often in the case law that's shaped IRS guidance), documented training, and a genuine business need for the property to be guarded, like a site with $80,000 in copper wiring and equipment sitting exposed overnight. We tell construction clients to keep a simple log: where the dog is stationed, what hours it works, and what it's protecting. If the IRS ever asks, that log is worth more than any receipt.

The same logic extends to a legitimate dog breeding operation run as an actual business, not a hobby. If you're filing a Schedule C, reporting income from litters, and running it with the intent to turn a profit, ordinary expenses like stud fees, veterinary costs, and advertising can be deducted under the same [Publication 535](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p535) standard. What sinks people here is treating it as a business only on the years there's a loss to claim. If your dogs live in the house, get treated like pets nine months a year, and the "business" has never turned a profit, that's a hobby, and hobby losses aren't deductible.

For a bar or restaurant client, we get this question in a different form: can the bar dog that greets regulars and lives behind the counter be a write-off? Almost always, no. A dog that improves ambiance or customer experience is a marketing choice, not a security or medical necessity, and it doesn't meet the ordinary-and-necessary test the way a guard dog patrolling a closed kitchen overnight might. If you run a hospitality business in Port Chester or anywhere in Westchester County and you're weighing this kind of expense, it's worth a conversation before you assume it qualifies. Our [restaurant clients](/industries/restaurants) come to us with plenty of gray-area expenses like this, and most of the time the fix is a small adjustment to how the expense is documented, not a denial.

## Service animals and the medical expense deduction

The clearest, best-documented deduction involving dogs has nothing to do with business at all. It's personal, and it's medical. [IRS Publication 502](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p502) allows taxpayers to deduct the cost of buying, training, and maintaining a guide dog or other service animal used to assist someone with a visual impairment, hearing impairment, or other physical disability. That includes food, grooming, and veterinary care necessary for the animal to stay healthy enough to perform its job.

This deduction lives on Schedule A as an itemized medical expense, which means two things in practice. First, you have to itemize instead of taking the standard deduction, and second, only the amount of your total medical expenses that exceeds 7.5% of your adjusted gross income actually counts. For a household with $120,000 in AGI, that threshold is $9,000, so a service dog alone rarely gets a taxpayer there. It usually needs to be stacked with other medical costs, like out-of-pocket treatment, prescriptions, or long-term care expenses, to make a real dent. If your household is navigating a mix of medical costs already, it's worth running the full picture rather than looking at the dog in isolation.

## Therapy dogs, emotional support animals, and where the line gets blurry

Here's where good intentions run into IRS reality. A therapy dog or emotional support animal is not automatically the same thing as a service animal for tax purposes. The IRS standard under Publication 502 is tied to a diagnosed physical or mental disability and a professionally prescribed need for the animal's specific trained function, not general companionship or stress relief. A letter from a doctor recommending a pet for anxiety, without more, usually isn't enough to support the deduction if it's challenged.

What tends to hold up is documentation showing the animal was trained for a specific task tied to a diagnosed condition, such as a psychiatric service dog trained to interrupt panic attacks or alert to specific symptoms, prescribed by a licensed professional as part of a treatment plan. What tends to fall apart is a beloved dog that happens to make its owner feel calmer. The emotional benefit is real. The tax deduction usually isn't, unless the medical and training documentation is specific enough to survive a second look.

## Keeping records that hold up if you're asked

Every legitimate dog-related deduction we've described shares one thing: paper trail. For a guard dog, that means a log of hours and location, training records, and a clear business reason the property needs protecting. For a breeding operation, it means running the numbers like a real business, with income, expenses, and a documented profit motive, not just deducting losses in the years it's convenient. For a service animal, it means medical documentation connecting the animal's training to a diagnosed condition, kept alongside your other itemized medical records.

We walk clients through this kind of documentation constantly, whether it's a construction company in Port Chester tracking site security costs or an individual client sorting out which medical expenses actually clear the itemization threshold. If you're weighing a dog-related deduction and you're not sure which bucket it falls into, that's exactly the kind of question worth asking before you file, not after you get a letter. ## Common Questions

**Can I deduct my dog's food and vet bills if I work from home?**
No. Owning a dog while running a home-based business doesn't make pet care a business expense. The dog would need a specific, documented business function, like guarding inventory or equipment, before any of its costs could qualify under [IRS Publication 535](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p535).

**Is a guard dog at my construction site really deductible?**
It can be, if the dog is trained for security work, actually stationed at the property, and the business has a real need for that protection, such as equipment or materials left on site overnight. Keep a log of the dog's hours and location, since that's the documentation that matters most if it's ever questioned.

**What's the difference between a service dog and a therapy dog for tax purposes?**
A service dog trained for a specific task tied to a diagnosed disability can qualify as a medical expense deduction under [IRS Publication 502](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p502). A therapy dog or emotional support animal generally doesn't qualify unless there's specific medical documentation showing prescribed training tied to a diagnosed condition.

**Can I deduct expenses for breeding dogs as a business?**
Yes, if you're running it as an actual business with a genuine profit motive, reported on Schedule C, and not simply claiming losses on what functions as a hobby. Consistent recordkeeping and a track record of trying to turn a profit matter more here than any single expense.
