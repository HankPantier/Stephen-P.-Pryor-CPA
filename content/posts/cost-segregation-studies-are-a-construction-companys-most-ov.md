A construction company finishes out a 40,000-square-foot warehouse for a client, then turns around and builds its own equipment yard and office three towns over. Nobody on that job stops to ask whether the electrical subpanels, the specialty flooring, or the site lighting really belong in a 39-year tax life or something much shorter. That question is a cost segregation study, and for construction companies, skipping it is one of the more expensive mistakes we see at our Port Chester office.

Most owners hear "cost segregation" and assume it's a real estate investor's tool, something for someone buying an apartment building or a strip mall. That assumption costs contractors real money every year.

## Real estate investors didn't invent this, they just talk about it more

Cost segregation studies break a building's cost into components with different depreciation lives under IRS rules: the structure itself over 39 years (or 27.5 for residential), but electrical work tied to specific equipment, certain plumbing, carpeting, cabinetry, parking lot paving, and landscaping often qualify for 5-, 7-, or 15-year lives instead. Real estate investors use this constantly because they buy finished buildings and want faster depreciation.

Construction companies are arguably better positioned to benefit. You're not buying a finished building from someone else and guessing at costs after the fact. You're constantly building, expanding, or buying facilities for your own operations, and you often self-perform the very work that creates the best reclassification opportunities. A general contractor who builds out a new shop, a specialty subcontractor who buys a warehouse for staging and storage, a paving company that constructs its own equipment yard: each of these is a cost segregation candidate that rarely gets the study done.

## Your job costing records are already halfway to the study

Here's the part most owners miss. A cost segregation study built by an engineering firm typically starts by reconstructing costs component by component, which is expensive and slow when the building was bought from someone else with no detailed records. Construction companies don't have that problem. If you self-performed part of the work, your own job costing system already tracks labor and materials by trade, by phase, by cost code.

That existing documentation makes the study faster, often cheaper, and considerably more defensible if the IRS ever asks questions. An engineer reviewing your project can point to your own invoices and time records for the electrical subcontractor, the specialty concrete work, or the site utilities, rather than estimating backward from a purchase price. Contractors who already deal with job costing complexity across multiple projects are sitting on the exact records that make this strategy work well.

## The building components that move from 39 years to 5

A typical study on a commercial or light industrial building reclassifies somewhere between 15% and 35% of total building cost into shorter recovery periods, depending on the type of facility. For construction companies building shops, yards, and offices, the categories usually include:

| Component | Standard life | Reclassified life |
|---|---|---|
| Structural shell, roof, foundation | 39 years | Stays at 39 years |
| Dedicated electrical for equipment | 39 years | 5 or 7 years |
| Specialty flooring, certain interior finishes | 39 years | 7 or 15 years |
| Parking lots, curbing, site lighting | 39 years | 15 years |
| Fencing, landscaping | 39 years | 15 years |

The categories that move to 5- and 7-year lives are the ones worth the most attention, because those are the assets that qualify for bonus depreciation right now, not just faster depreciation over time.

## Bonus depreciation is shrinking, which makes timing urgent

Bonus depreciation let businesses write off 100% of qualifying short-life property in the year it was placed in service through 2022. That percentage dropped to 80% in 2023, 60% in 2024, and sits at 40% for property placed in service in 2025. It's scheduled to fall to 20% in 2026 and disappear in 2027 unless Congress changes course again.

Every year a construction company waits to identify its 5- and 7-year property is a year that same property qualifies for a smaller bonus percentage. A facility placed in service in 2024 captured 60% bonus on reclassified assets. The identical building placed in service in 2026 captures 20%. The tax law didn't change what qualifies. It changed how much of it you get to deduct immediately, and that window is closing on a fixed schedule.

## A rough number to make this concrete

Say a contractor buys and renovates a $1.5 million facility for its own equipment storage and office use, excluding land value. A study identifies 22% of that cost, roughly $330,000, as 5-, 7-, or 15-year property instead of 39-year property. At 40% bonus depreciation, that's a first-year deduction of about $132,000 on the reclassified assets alone, on top of standard depreciation on the rest of the building.

Compare that to depreciating the entire $1.5 million evenly over 39 years, which produces roughly $38,000 a year regardless of what's actually inside the walls. For a construction company already dealing with thin margins between project phases and uneven cash flow, that gap between $132,000 and $38,000 in year one is the kind of number that changes what's possible with equipment purchases, hiring, or paying down debt.

## Common Questions

**Does a cost segregation study only apply to buildings I own, or can it apply to a building I lease and improve?**
It generally applies to buildings you own or to leasehold improvements you pay for and capitalize, even if you don't own the underlying real estate. If your construction company built out a leased shop or office and paid for those improvements directly, that cost may still qualify for a study.

**How much does a cost segregation study cost, and is it worth it for a smaller facility?**
Studies typically run a few thousand dollars into the low tens of thousands depending on building size and complexity, and they usually make sense once building costs (excluding land) exceed roughly $500,000. Below that, the tax savings may not outweigh the study cost, though it's worth running the numbers on a specific project rather than assuming.

**Will a cost segregation study increase my audit risk?**
A properly documented, engineering-based study is a standard, IRS-recognized method, not a red flag by itself. The risk comes from sloppy or unsupported studies, which is exactly why using your own detailed job costing records as backup matters so much for construction companies specifically.

**Can I do a cost segregation study on a building I bought or built years ago?**
Yes, through a process called a look-back study, which lets you claim the missed depreciation in the current year without amending prior returns. It's worth reviewing any building placed in service in the last several years that never went through this process.
