## Why summer camp costs can work in your favor at tax time

If you're a working parent in Port Chester or anywhere else in Westchester County, summer camp isn't just a scheduling solution, it's often a legitimate tax break hiding in plain sight. The dependent care tax credit was built for exactly this situation: parents who need supervised care for a child under 13 so they can work, and who pay for it out of pocket. Day camp tuition frequently qualifies, yet plenty of families never claim it because the rules feel murky or they assume camp costs don't count. They often do. A quick conversation with a CPA who knows the details can mean the difference between leaving money on the table and turning a summer expense into a real credit on your return.

<!-- block: checklist-section | variant: with-image | image: parent-dropping-child-summer-camp.jpg | alt: "Parent dropping off child at a day camp drop-off line" | query: "parent child summer camp drop off" -->
## Which camps and expenses qualify

The IRS draws a firm line here: day camps qualify, overnight camps do not, even if it's the same organization running both programs. The logic is simple. The credit exists to cover care that lets you work during the day, not lodging. Here's a quick self-check before you file:

- The camp operates during the day and does not include overnight stays
- The child is under 13, or is a dependent unable to care for themselves
- The camp expense enabled you (and your spouse, if filing jointly) to work or look for work
- You have the camp provider's name, address, and taxpayer ID number
- Specialty camps count too. Sports, art, coding, and academic day camps generally qualify the same way a traditional day camp does

A common misconception: parents assume only traditional summer camps qualify. In reality, before- and after-school care programs and even summer day programs run by a local YMCA or municipal recreation department can count, as long as the daytime-only rule holds.

## How much you could save with the dependent care credit

The math isn't complicated once you see it laid out. You can count up to $3,000 in qualifying expenses for one child, or $6,000 for two or more children, in a given tax year. The credit itself is a percentage of that amount, ranging from 20% to 35% depending on your adjusted gross income, with higher-income households landing at the lower end of that range. In practical terms, that means the credit could put anywhere from $600 to $2,100 back in your pocket for two kids at camp, depending on your income and expenses. It's not a deduction that only shaves a little off taxable income. It's a direct, dollar-for-dollar reduction of what you owe, which is why it's worth getting right. ## Dependent care FSA versus the tax credit

Many Westchester County parents have access to a dependent care flexible spending account through their employer, and it raises a fair question: FSA or tax credit, or both? You can use an FSA to set aside up to $5,000 pretax for dependent care, which lowers your taxable income right away. The catch is you can't double-dip. Any camp expenses paid through the FSA can't also be claimed for the credit, and the $3,000/$6,000 expense caps for the credit get reduced by whatever you ran through the FSA. For some families, the FSA wins because it lowers income before payroll taxes even apply. For others, especially those in a lower tax bracket where the credit's percentage runs higher, claiming the credit outright makes more sense. This is a household-by-household calculation, and it's exactly the kind of decision that benefits from sitting down with someone who can run both scenarios side by side.

## Record-keeping tips for Westchester County families

If the IRS ever asks questions, documentation is what settles them quickly and without stress. Keep these on hand throughout the year, not just at tax time:

- Itemized camp receipts or invoices showing dates of attendance and amounts paid
- The camp provider's tax identification number (EIN or Social Security number for an individual provider)
- Proof that both parents worked or were actively job-searching during the camp period
- Bank or credit card statements matching the camp payments, in case a receipt goes missing
- A written note if a camp offers both day and overnight programs, clarifying which portion you're claiming

Good habits here pay off well beyond one tax season. Denise Romano, CPA, and the rest of our team see these questions come up every summer, and having the paperwork organized in advance makes filing faster and audit exposure lower.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Summer Camps May Qualify for Dependent Care Tax Credit | Stephen P. Pryor, CPA

**Q: Does overnight summer camp qualify for the dependent care tax credit?**
A: No. The IRS only allows day camp expenses toward the dependent care credit. Overnight camps, even ones run by the same organization as a day program, are excluded because the credit is meant to cover care that allows parents to work during the day, not lodging.

**Q: How much is the dependent care tax credit worth for summer camp?**
A: You can count up to $3,000 in expenses for one child or $6,000 for two or more. The credit equals 20% to 35% of that amount depending on your adjusted gross income, meaning potential savings of $600 to $2,100 for two children.

**Q: Can I use a dependent care FSA and claim the tax credit for the same camp expenses?**
A: Not for the same dollars. Any amount run through a dependent care FSA reduces the expense cap available for the credit. Depending on your income and tax bracket, one option may save more than the other, which is worth reviewing with a CPA.

**Q: What records do I need to claim summer camp on my taxes?**
A: Keep itemized camp receipts, the provider's tax ID number, and proof that the expense allowed you to work. Bank statements matching payments are useful backup if a receipt is ever lost or questioned.
