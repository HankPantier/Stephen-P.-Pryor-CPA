## Why proactive tax planning matters more than ever

If your tax bill keeps surprising you every April, you're not alone. Federal brackets shift, New York State keeps its own rules, and Westchester County property and local taxes add another layer on top. Waiting until tax season to think about any of it means you've already missed most of your options.

Proactive tax planning flips that script. Instead of reacting to a number your accountant hands you in March, you're making decisions in June, September, and December that shape what that number looks like. For business owners in Port Chester running construction crews, managing rental properties, or pouring drinks behind a bar, that difference can mean thousands of dollars kept in the business instead of sent to the IRS.

<!-- block: content-split | variant: image-right | image: contractor-reviewing-tax-documents.jpg | alt: "Construction business owner reviewing financial paperwork with a calculator" | query: "contractor reviewing paperwork office" -->
## Step one: review your entity structure and income timing

How your business is legally structured, sole proprietorship, LLC, S-corp, or partnership, determines how much you pay in self-employment tax, what deductions are available, and how income flows to your personal return. A lot of construction and real estate owners set up their entity years ago and never revisit it, even as revenue grows or ownership changes.

Income timing matters just as much. Deferring a large invoice into January, accelerating a deductible equipment purchase into December, or timing a property sale around other income can shift your effective tax rate meaningfully. These aren't one-size-fits-all moves. What works for a two-person plumbing business looks different from what works for a real estate investor holding three rental properties. Our [entity type analysis](/what-we-do/entity-type-analysis) work walks through the state and federal tradeoffs before you commit to a structure, not after.

<!-- block: checklist-section | variant: with-image-left | image: restaurant-owner-financial-review.jpg | alt: "Restaurant owner reviewing sales and tax records at a bar counter" | query: "restaurant owner reviewing financial records" -->
## Step two: maximize deductions and industry-specific credits

This is where most of the money gets left on the table. General deduction advice misses the details that actually matter in your industry.

- **Construction:** Job costing that separates labor, materials, and overhead per project, plus depreciation strategy on trucks, tools, and heavy equipment
- **Real estate:** Cost segregation studies that accelerate depreciation, and 1031 exchange timing that defers capital gains without tripping IRS rules
- **Bars and restaurants:** Tip credit calculations, proper cash handling documentation, and sales tax nuances that vary depending on what's served and how

A generalist tax preparer might catch the basics. A CPA who's worked inside these industries catches the rest. If you've never had someone walk through your books looking specifically for construction, real estate, or hospitality deductions, there's a good chance you're overpaying.

<!-- block: content-split | variant: image-left | image: retirement-planning-discussion.jpg | alt: "Small business owner discussing retirement plan options with an advisor" | query: "business owner retirement planning meeting" -->
## Step three: plan for retirement and long-term tax efficiency

Retirement contributions are one of the few tax moves that reduce your bill today while building something for later. The problem is choosing the right plan. A Solo 401(k), SEP IRA, or SIMPLE IRA each come with different contribution limits, deadlines, and administrative requirements, and the right fit depends on your income, whether you have employees, and how much cash flow flexibility you need.

For a construction business owner with fluctuating annual income, or a real estate investor managing multiple entities, the wrong plan can mean lower contribution limits than necessary or paperwork headaches that outweigh the tax benefit. Our [retirement plan analysis](/what-we-do/retirement-plan-analysis) service matches the plan to your actual numbers, not a generic recommendation.

## Frequently Asked Questions About 4 Steps to Get Ahead of Higher Taxes | Stephen P. Pryor, CPA

**Q: What is proactive tax planning and how is it different from tax preparation?**
A: Tax preparation is filing your return based on decisions already made. Proactive tax planning happens throughout the year, adjusting entity structure, income timing, and deductions before December 31 so your tax bill reflects choices you made on purpose, not ones you missed.

**Q: How does entity structure affect my tax bill in New York?**
A: Your entity type, whether LLC, S-corp, or partnership, determines self-employment tax exposure, available deductions, and how income passes to your personal return. Construction and real estate owners often outgrow their original structure without realizing it, leaving savings unclaimed year after year.

**Q: What tax deductions do restaurants and bars commonly miss?**
A: Tip credit calculations, proper cash handling documentation, and sales tax nuances tied to what's sold are frequently overlooked. Without industry-specific review, restaurant and bar owners often underclaim credits available to them or misreport figures that trigger compliance issues later.

**Q: How can a retirement plan lower my business taxes?**
A: Contributions to a Solo 401(k), SEP IRA, or SIMPLE IRA reduce taxable income in the year you contribute while building long-term savings. The right plan depends on your income pattern and whether you have employees, which is why a plan comparison matters before you set one up.
