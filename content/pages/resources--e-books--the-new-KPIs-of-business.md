## Why the right numbers matter more than ever

A profit and loss statement tells you what happened last month. It doesn't tell you whether your bar's pour cost is creeping up, whether a construction job is tracking to budget, or whether a rental property still clears the cash-on-cash return you need to justify holding it. That's the gap between financial reporting and financial management.

Businesses that grow with intention track numbers that predict trouble or opportunity, not just the ones that show up at tax time. With a secure online platform giving you real-time access to your own financial data, you can check job costing on a Tuesday afternoon instead of waiting for a year-end statement to tell you a project lost money three months ago.

This guide covers the KPIs we ask construction, real estate, and hospitality clients to watch closely, along with the core metrics every business owner in Westchester County and beyond should have on a dashboard somewhere.

## Core financial KPIs every business should monitor

Before getting into industry specifics, every business we work with, from a general contractor in Port Chester to a landlord managing five properties, should have visibility into a handful of foundational metrics.

- **Gross margin**: what's left after direct costs, tracked monthly rather than just at tax season, so a slow slide doesn't go unnoticed for six months.
- **Cash flow trend**: the direction of cash in and out over a rolling 13 weeks, which says more about survival than the balance in your account today.
- **Overhead ratio**: fixed costs as a percentage of revenue, flagging when spending growth is outpacing sales growth.
- **Labor cost percentage**: payroll and related costs measured against revenue, one of the fastest-moving numbers in construction, property management, and restaurants alike.

These four won't tell you everything. But they're the baseline. If they're not being tracked monthly, through bookkeeping that's current instead of three months behind, everything below is guesswork.

## KPIs for construction businesses

Construction cash flow is lumpy by nature. Money comes in tied to draws and milestones, while labor and materials go out on a different schedule entirely. Four KPIs help general contractors and subcontractors keep that timing from turning into a crisis.

Job costing accuracy matters most: knowing actual cost against estimate for every job, updated weekly rather than reconstructed after the fact. Work-in-progress reporting comes next, showing billed versus earned revenue on active jobs so overbilling or underbilling gets caught before it distorts the financials. Backlog value, the total contracted work not yet completed, shows what's coming six months out and whether it's time to staff up or slow down. Profitability per project, tracked separately from company-wide margin, reveals which types of jobs actually make money and which ones just keep crews busy.

Our [construction accounting work](/industries/construction) is built around these four numbers because they predict a cash crunch before it happens, not after.

## KPIs for real estate investors and businesses

Real estate performance isn't just about whether a property is profitable this year. It's about whether it's performing relative to what was paid, what was financed, and what the next move should be.

Cap rate shows a property's return independent of financing, useful for comparing deals or deciding whether to sell. Cash-on-cash return factors in actual financing and shows what's really being earned on the cash invested, which is the number that pays the mortgage and the bills. Depreciation tracking, especially through cost segregation studies, affects the tax bill every year a property is held, and missing it means leaving money with the IRS instead of in the bank. 1031 exchange readiness, meaning clean books and accurate basis records before they're needed, determines whether a like-kind exchange can move quickly without triggering an unplanned tax hit.

Investors and property managers who track these across [multiple entities](/industries/real-estate) make faster, better-informed decisions about when to hold, refinance, or sell.

## KPIs for bars and restaurants

Bars and restaurants run on thin margins, often 3 to 5 percent net, which means small leaks sink the whole operation fast. The KPIs that matter here are the ones that catch a leak before it becomes a hole.

Prime cost, the combined total of cost of goods sold and labor, is the single number that predicts profitability better than any other in hospitality; most operators aim to keep it under 60 percent of sales. Pour cost tracks liquor, beer, and wine costs against bar revenue specifically, exposing over-pouring, waste, or theft that a general COGS number would hide. Labor-to-sales ratio, tracked by shift or daypart, shows whether a slow Tuesday is overstaffed or a busy Friday is understaffed. Sales tax compliance, including accurate tip reporting and cash handling procedures, protects owners from the kind of audit exposure that catches bar and pub operators off guard.
