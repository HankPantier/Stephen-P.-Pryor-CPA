## Why small businesses in Port Chester are prime cybersecurity targets

Ask any restaurant owner in Port Chester whether hackers are interested in a business with a dozen employees and a single register, and most will laugh off the idea. That assumption is exactly what makes construction firms, real estate offices, and neighborhood bars such reliable targets. Cyber criminals don't chase size, they chase gaps: no dedicated IT staff, shared logins, and point-of-sale systems nobody has updated since 2019. Small operators in Westchester County rarely have the cash reserves to absorb a six-figure recovery bill, which is what separates a bad week from a business-ending one. This isn't a problem for your IT guy to solve later. A breach touches payroll, vendor payments, and tax filings, which makes it a financial risk first and a technical one second. Treating cybersecurity like a bookkeeping line item, something you budget for and review regularly, is the more realistic approach for a construction crew or a two-location restaurant group.

## The financial and tax risks of a data breach

A data breach rarely stays contained to a computer screen. Once payroll files, bank account numbers, or client W-9s are exposed, you're looking at fraudulent tax filings, unauthorized wire transfers, and clients who no longer trust you with their financial details. For a construction company juggling subcontractor payments or a bar owner processing nightly credit card batches, that exposure compounds fast. This is where a CPA who already knows your [bookkeeping](/what-we-do/bookkeeping) and [payroll](/what-we-do/payroll) systems has an edge over a generic IT vendor. Stephen Pryor, Shawn Wilson, and Denise Romano review client books closely enough to notice when a vendor invoice looks off, a payroll run doesn't match hours logged, or a bank reconciliation turns up a transaction nobody recognizes. Catching those anomalies early is often what keeps a security incident from becoming a tax problem too.

## Low cost cybersecurity steps any business owner can take today

You don't need a six-figure IT budget to close the gaps that hackers exploit most. Most of these steps cost nothing beyond the time it takes to set them up, and they work whether you're running a two-person real estate office or a restaurant with a dozen part-time servers.

- Use a password manager so employees stop reusing the same login across your point-of-sale system, email, and bank accounts.
- Turn on two-factor authentication for banking, payroll, and email logins; it stops most stolen-password attacks cold.
- Set software and point-of-sale systems to update automatically instead of waiting for a "later" that never comes.
- Train employees on spotting phishing emails during onboarding, and again once a year, not just once.
- Back up financial records and job files to a second location, whether that's cloud storage or an external drive kept off-site.
- Limit who can access payroll and banking logins to the people who actually need them.

## Industry-specific risks for construction, real estate, and restaurant businesses

### Construction
icon: Hammer

Job-site laptops and tablets often connect to public Wi-Fi at multiple locations, and subcontractor payment details get shared over email without a second thought. A lost device or a spoofed invoice email can drain a project's cash flow before a job costing report even catches it. See how we support [construction businesses](/industries/construction) day to day.

### Real Estate
icon: Home

Real estate offices store closing documents, social security numbers, and wire instructions, exactly the data wire fraud schemes target. A single spoofed email asking a buyer to "update" wiring instructions has cost firms hundreds of thousands of dollars. Our work with [real estate clients](/industries/real-estate) includes flagging these exposure points during onboarding.

### Bars and Restaurants
icon: Wallet

Point-of-sale systems and tip reporting create a steady flow of card data and cash handling records, both attractive to thieves and both subject to compliance rules. Outdated POS software is one of the most common entry points for restaurant breaches. See how we work with [restaurant and bar owners](/industries/restaurants) on this.
