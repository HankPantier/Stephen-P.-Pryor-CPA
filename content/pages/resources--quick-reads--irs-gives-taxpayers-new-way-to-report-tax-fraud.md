## IRS rolls out a simpler way to report tax fraud and scams

The IRS recently streamlined how taxpayers and business owners report suspected fraud, phishing attempts, and tax scams. Instead of sorting through a maze of forms to figure out where a complaint belongs, filers now have a more direct channel for flagging suspicious activity, whether that's a fake charity solicitation, a text message posing as the IRS, or a preparer inflating deductions without your knowledge.

For business owners in Port Chester, NY, this update lands at a useful time. Tax season brings a predictable wave of scam attempts aimed at construction crews, restaurant staff, and property managers alike. A faster reporting path means less time spent figuring out who to call and more time getting a fraudulent notice or scam text in front of the right people. [Stephen P. Pryor, CPA](/who-we-are/stephen-pryor) tracks changes like this one so Westchester County clients don't have to.

## Why tax fraud and scam awareness matters for local businesses and individuals

[Construction companies](/industries/construction) juggling multiple subcontractors face a specific kind of exposure: fake W-9 requests, inflated invoices, and payroll scams that mimic a real vendor's email address down to the letter. [Real estate businesses](/industries/real-estate) handling closings and 1031 exchanges are frequent targets for wire fraud schemes that impersonate title companies or the IRS itself. [Bars and restaurants](/industries/restaurants), with cash-heavy operations and tip reporting already under scrutiny, see a steady stream of phishing attempts aimed squarely at bookkeepers and payroll staff.

None of this is unique to Manhattan or Brooklyn. Port Chester and the surrounding Westchester County business community see the same scam patterns, just with fewer resources aimed at catching them early. A restaurant manager who wires money based on a spoofed email, or a contractor who pays a fake subcontractor invoice, loses real cash with little chance of getting it back. Awareness is the cheapest protection a business has.

## What to do if you suspect fraud or receive a suspicious notice

### Verify before you respond
The IRS does not initiate contact by text, email, or social media asking for payment. A phone call to a number you look up independently, not one printed on the suspicious notice, is the safest first move.

### Document everything
Save the email, screenshot the text, and keep the notice itself. Details like sender addresses and dates matter if you need to file a report later.

### Report it to the right place
Suspicious IRS-related activity gets reported directly to the IRS through its updated reporting channel. Scams involving your bank or payroll provider should also go to them directly.

### Call your CPA before you act
Before wiring money, replying to a notice, or changing payroll information based on an email request, get a second set of eyes on it. A quick call can save you from an expensive mistake.
