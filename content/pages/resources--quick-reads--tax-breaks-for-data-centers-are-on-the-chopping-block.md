## States are reconsidering data center tax incentives

A handful of states are pulling back on the tax breaks they once handed data center operators, and the reasoning is straightforward: the credits cost more than lawmakers expected, and the jobs promised didn't always show up. New York hasn't been immune to this kind of reversal. Sales tax exemptions and property tax abatements tied to data centers are getting a second look in several statehouses this year.

The bigger story here isn't about server farms. It's about how quickly a tax incentive that looked permanent can disappear. Business owners who built a tax strategy around a specific credit, exemption, or abatement need someone checking whether that benefit still exists next filing season, not just this one.

## Construction, real estate, and hospitality owners feel these shifts too

Data centers aren't the only businesses that lean on state and local incentives. Construction firms in Westchester County often factor in depreciation schedules and project-based credits when bidding jobs. Real estate owners plan around 1031 exchanges and cost segregation studies. Bars and restaurants count on payroll tax credits tied to hiring and, in some states, tip reporting relief.

When legislators revisit one industry's tax breaks, it's usually a signal they're reviewing the whole incentive structure. A property tax abatement your construction company relies on, or a sales tax exemption your restaurant counts on, can get quietly narrowed or sunset in the same budget cycle. If nobody's watching the calendar, you find out when the bill lands, not before.

## How construction and real estate businesses can stay ahead of changing tax rules

Entity structure decisions made five years ago don't always hold up once tax law shifts. A construction company organized as an S-corp might benefit from a different structure once New York adjusts depreciation rules or project-based credits. Real estate owners face similar exposure: cost segregation opportunities and 1031 exchange rules change enough that a plan built in 2022 can leave money on the table in 2026.

Our entity type analysis and ongoing advisory work exist for exactly this reason. We review entity structure, depreciation strategy, and applicable New York incentives on a recurring basis, not once at onboarding. That's how a construction client avoids overpaying because nobody flagged a new state credit, and how a real estate owner catches a cost segregation opportunity before the filing deadline instead of after.

## What this means for bars, restaurants, and other local businesses

Bars and restaurants run on thin margins, and the tax rules that touch them move often: sales tax on prepared food, tip credit reporting, payroll-based hiring incentives. A change in any one of these can shift a bar's monthly numbers more than owners expect, especially when cash handling and tip reporting already demand close attention.

Year-round planning catches these shifts as they happen. Instead of finding out at tax time that a sales tax rule changed six months earlier, a hospitality client gets a heads-up when it's still useful. That's the difference between adjusting pricing or staffing proactively and scrambling to cover a surprise liability in April.
